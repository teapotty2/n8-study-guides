Burns (Chapter 57)

▼

1.1 Epidemiology & Burn Depth Classification

▼

Epidemiology

  • 486,000 treated annually in the US; ~40,000 hospitalized
  • Causes: 41% flame, 35% scalds, 10% contact, 3% electrical, 3% chemical, 3% inhalation only
  • 73% occur in the home; men >2× more likely than women
  • Adults over 60 = 15% of hospitalized burns; thinner, less elastic skin → deeper burns, slower healing; mortality ~13% vs 2.9% overall
  • Most common complications in elderly: pneumonia, UTI, respiratory failure

Burn Depth — The Four Degrees

DegreeLayers AffectedAppearancePain?Treatment
1st — Superficial Epidermis only Red, blanches with pressure, dry, possible blisters Yes — tingling, hyperesthesia Cool compresses, lubricants; NO topical antimicrobials; heals in days
2nd — Partial Thickness Epidermis + partial dermis; appendages intact Blistered, mottled red, weeping, edema Yes — sensitive to air currents 2–3 weeks; may need grafting
3rd — Full Thickness Epidermis + full dermis; nerve fibers destroyed Dry, pale/white/red-brown/charred; leathery; visible coagulated vessels NO — insensate Grafting required; scarring, loss of function
4th Deep tissue: fat, fascia, muscle, bone Charred Shock, myoglobinuria, hemolysis Amputation likely; grafting not beneficial
Exam Focus

3rd-degree burns are INSENSATE — absence of pain is a finding, not reassurance. Full-thickness destruction eliminates nerve fibers. Distractors try to make you reassure the patient that "it doesn't hurt so it's not that bad."

TBSA Estimation Methods

  • Rule of Nines: Head 9%, each arm 9%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, each leg 18%, perineum 1%
  • Lund & Browder: Most precise; adjusts head/leg percentages for age; reassess within 72 hours
  • Palmar method: Patient's own hand (with fingers) = 1% TBSA; use for scattered burns

ABA Burn Center Referral Criteria

  • Partial-thickness ≥10% TBSA
  • Burns of face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum, major joints
  • Third-degree burns (any size)
  • Electrical burns (including lightning), chemical burns, inhalation injury
  • Preexisting medical disorders; concomitant trauma
  • Children in non-pediatric facilities; special social/emotional/rehab needs

Factors Determining Burn Depth

  1. How injury occurred
  2. Causative agent (flame vs. scalding liquid)
  3. Temperature of agent
  4. Duration of contact
  5. Thickness of skin at injury site

1.2 Pathophysiology — Zones of Injury & Systemic Effects

▼

Zones of Burn Injury

  • Zone of Coagulation (center) — maximum destruction; cellular death; irreversible
  • Zone of Stasis (surrounding) — injured but possibly viable cells; ischemia → necrosis in 24–48h WITHOUT intervention — your nursing actions here matter most
  • Zone of Hyperemia (outermost) — minimal injury; spontaneous recovery

Systemic Effects — Burns >20% TBSA

Cardiovascular
  • Immediate ↓CO (before plasma loss begins)
  • Plasma shifts → ↓BP → burn shock (hypovolemic type)
  • Compensatory: ↑HR + vasoconstriction
  • ↑Hematocrit = hemoconcentration
  • Peak capillary leak: 6–8h post-injury
  • Diuresis begins 48+ h as capillaries heal
Fluid & Electrolytes
  • Immediate: Hyperkalemia (cell destruction) + Hyponatremia (Na lost in edema)
  • Later: hypokalemia possible
  • Edema peaks 4–18h post-injury
Pulmonary
  • Upper airway (above glottis): obstructive edema can take UP TO 48H to develop
  • Lower airway: chemical injury → ↓surfactant, bronchospasm, secretions
  • CO poisoning: binds Hgb 200× more than O&sub2; → SpO&sub2; UNRELIABLE
  • Cyanide: lactic acidosis after resuscitation = tipoff
  • Dx inhalation injury: bronchoscopy (gold standard)
Renal
  • Vasoconstriction → ↓GFR → oliguria
  • Myoglobin (muscle damage) → red urine → ATN/AKI if flow inadequate
  • Free Hgb from RBC destruction also in urine
GI
  • Paralytic ileus (>20–25% TBSA) — NG tube needed
  • Curling's ulcer: gastric/duodenal erosion from ischemia; "coffee-ground" emesis, occult blood
  • Bacterial translocation from mucosal barrier loss
Other
  • Thermoregulation: skin loss → hypothermia early
  • Immune: largest barrier lost → massive infection risk
Prof Emphasis

Curling's ulcer = gastric erosion from severe burns; severe complication from ischemia and cell necrosis. Named complication your professor specifically called out — know it.

1.3 Phases of Burn Care

▼
Emergent / Resuscitative
Injury → completion of fluid resuscitation
  • ABCDE primary survey
  • Prevent shock
  • Prevent respiratory distress
  • Detect concomitant injuries
  • Wound assessment
Acute / Intermediate
Diuresis begins → near wound closure (48–72h+)
  • Wound care + closure
  • Infection prevention (highest risk)
  • Nutritional support
  • Pain management
  • Early mobility
Rehabilitation
Major wound closure → optimal adjustment
  • Prevent/treat scars + contractures
  • PT / OT / vocational rehab
  • Functional/cosmetic reconstruction
  • Psychosocial counseling
Prof Emphasis

Know these phases, what's happening in each one, and what the priority nursing actions are. This is a reliable exam framework — questions will be anchored to phase.

1.4 Emergent Phase — Pre-Hospital & Hospital Management

▼

Scene Care — Priority Sequence

  1. Prevent injury to rescuer
  2. Stop burning: extinguish flames / cool water / irrigate chemicals
  3. Establish ABCs
  4. Start O&sub2; and large-bore IVs (through burned tissue if needed)
  5. Remove restrictive objects (clothing, jewelry, piercings) — edema develops fast
  6. Assess and obtain history
  7. Treat as C-spine injury for falls and electrical burns
Prof Emphasis

Give O&sub2; even if SpO&sub2; looks fine. Start IVs immediately — even through burned tissue if necessary. Don't delay.

Scene Rules

  • Stop, drop, roll for flames
  • NEVER apply ice — worsens tissue damage, causes hypothermia
  • Cool water is best first aid; brief cool towel soaks (NOT ice; NOT prolonged cold soaks)
  • For dry chemicals: brush off first, THEN rinse with water
  • Chemical eye exposure: flush copiously with cool clean water immediately
  • No ointments or salves at scene

Fluid Resuscitation (ABA Formula)

Thermal / Chemical Burns
2 mL × kg × %TBSA (2nd + 3rd + 4th degree)
Electrical Burns
4 mL × kg × %TBSA
  • Fluid of choice: Lactated Ringer's (pH and osmolality closest to plasma)
  • Timing is critical: Count from TIME OF INJURY, not ED arrival
  • ½ of total volume in first 8 hours
  • Remaining ½ over the next 16 hours
  • Total is a 24-hour calculation
Exam Focus — Most Common Distractor

The timer starts at time of injury — NOT when the patient arrived at the ED. If a patient was burned at 10 AM and arrives at noon, the first 8-hour window already started 2 hours ago. This is the #1 tested distractor.

Urine Output Targets (Resuscitation Adequacy)

  • Thermal/Chemical: 0.5–1 mL/kg/hr
  • Electrical: 75–100 mL/hr (until urine clears; red urine = myoglobinuria)
  • UO is the standard for assessing resuscitation — BP and HR alone are NOT reliable in burns

Over vs. Under Resuscitation

  • Over: Heart failure, pulmonary edema
  • Under: MODS, shock, ischemia, organ hypofunction

Other Hospital Priorities

  • Foley catheter for fluid monitoring
  • NG tube if >20–25% TBSA or intubated (prevent aspiration/ileus)
  • IV analgesics ONLY (poor tissue perfusion makes IM/SQ unreliable)
  • ECG for electrical burns + continuous cardiac monitoring
  • Tetanus prophylaxis (burn = contaminated wound)
  • Psychological support to patient and family

Emergent Phase Complications

  1. Acute Respiratory Failure — upper airway edema can take 48h to manifest
  2. Distributive Shock — from systemic inflammatory mediators
  3. Acute Kidney Injury — hypovolemia + myoglobin + hemoglobin in tubules
  4. Compartment Syndrome — circumferential burns → escharotomy/fasciotomy
  5. Paralytic Ileus — decreased GI motility → NG decompression
  6. Curling's Ulcer — gastric stress ulcer from ischemia
Exam Focus

NEVER add KCl to burn IVF — hyperkalemia already present from massive cell destruction.

NEVER use NS as primary resuscitation for burns — causes hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.

1.5 Wound Care, Topical Agents & Grafting

▼

Debridement Types

  1. Natural (autolytic): Spontaneous separation; weeks to months
  2. Mechanical: Surgical tools; removes eschar to point of pain/bleeding
    • NEVER wet-to-dry for burns — tears off new granulation tissue
    • Use wet-to-wet or wet-to-moist ONLY
  3. Chemical (enzymatic): Topical enzymatic agents
  4. Surgical: Excision when hemodynamically stable and edema resolved

Topical Antimicrobial Agents

AgentPenetrates Eschar?Key Notes
Silver sulfadiazine 1% (Silvadene) NO Broad spectrum; forms pseudoeschar; most common first-line agent
Mafenide acetate 5% (Sulfamylon) YES — ONLY ONE Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor → metabolic acidosis; painful on application
Silver nitrate 0.5% solution NO Hypotonic → leaches Na¹ and K¹ from wound; stains everything black
Silver-impregnated dressings Varies Can leave in place for days; lower frequency of changes
Exam Focus — Most Tested Topical Agent Fact

Mafenide acetate (Sulfamylon) is the ONLY topical antimicrobial that penetrates eschar. All others act on the surface. This is a standalone exam question and appears in multiple distractor sets.

Skin Grafting

  • Autograft (gold standard): Patient's own skin; no rejection; split-thickness in sheets or meshed
    • First dressing change: 3–5 days post-op
    • Graft loss causes: infection, bleeding beneath graft, shearing
  • Homograft (allograft): Cadaver skin; temporary; WILL be rejected
    • Purulent exudate = notify provider; may need removal — do NOT independently debride
  • Xenograft (heterograft): Pigskin; temporary
    • Separating xenograft = trim away — EXPECTED as wound heals, not an emergency
  • Elastic pressure garments: Worn CONTINUOUSLY (24h/day) for hypertrophic scarring prevention
Exam Focus — Graft Behavior

Xenograft (pigskin) separating = trim away; expected. Homograft with purulent exudate = notify provider. Don't mix these up.

1.6 Pain Management & Nutritional Support

▼

Pain Management

  • Types: Background/resting, Procedural, Breakthrough
  • Premedicate BEFORE all wound care and dressing changes — always
  • IV route only during emergent and acute phases (poor perfusion in other tissues)
  • Opioids: morphine, fentanyl; titrate to effect
  • Nonpharmacologic (CAM): music, positioning, aromatherapy, distraction, acupuncture
  • Post-burn itching: antipruritic agents, skin lubrication, pat — DO NOT scratch
  • Burn pain = one of the most severe forms of acute pain → highest-priority nursing diagnosis

Nutritional Support

  • Burns create profound hypermetabolism — caloric demands skyrocket
  • Enteral route preferred over parenteral whenever possible
  • Jejunal feedings often used (lower aspiration risk)
  • Carbohydrates = most important energy source for burn patients
  • Goal: nitrogen balance for wound healing
  • Start ASAP — early enteral feeding reduces infection risk
Exam Focus

Weight loss in the acute/rehab phase = fat catabolism from hypermetabolism, NOT fluid loss. Fluid shifts are earlier; metabolic weight loss is ongoing.

Emergency Nursing (Chapter 67)

2.1 Core Principles & ESI Triage

▼

Core Principles

  • The ED is whatever the patient considers an emergency — no gatekeeping
  • EMTALA: every ED with Medicare agreement must screen all patients with emergency complaint
  • 139 million ED visits/year (2017); 40 million injury-related
  • Emergency nurse establishes priorities, continuously assesses, supports families, educates

Legal Issues

  • Consent required unless unconscious/critical — document LOC and status when unconscious
  • Paper bags (NOT plastic) for clothing/evidence — plastic retains moisture, destroys forensic evidence
  • Treat unconscious patient AS IF conscious

ESI Triage System (5 Levels)

Based on: acuity AND anticipated resource needs

LevelCategoryReassessDefinition
1ResuscitationContinuousImmediate threat to life
2Emergentq15 minHigh-risk; confused/lethargic; severe pain/distress
3Urgentq30 minStable but needs multiple resources
4Less urgentq60 minStable; needs one resource
5Non-urgentq120 minStable; no resources needed

Triage decision target: <5 minutes

Exam Focus — ED Triage vs. Disaster Triage

ED Triage: Most critically ill get the most resources — regardless of outcome probability.

Disaster Triage: Greatest good for the greatest number — likelihood of survival guides resource allocation. These are deliberately different frameworks. Confusing them = wrong answer.

2.2 Primary Survey (ABCDE) & Secondary Survey

▼

Primary Survey — ABCDE

LetterFocusKey Actions
AAirwayEstablish patent airway; protect cervical spine
BBreathingAdequate ventilation; bilateral breath sounds; O&sub2;
CCirculationControl hemorrhage; prevent/treat shock; check peripheral pulses; prevent hypothermia
DDisabilityGCS + AVPU (Alert, Verbal, Pain, Unresponsive)
EExposureUndress completely to assess all wounds and injuries

Secondary Survey

  • Complete health history; head-to-toe reassessment; vital signs
  • Diagnostic/lab testing; ECG; arterial lines; urinary catheters
  • Splinting fractures; wound care and closure

Priority Emergency Measures — Hierarchy

  1. Airway obstruction → establish airway and ventilation
  2. Hemorrhage → control bleeding
  3. Hypovolemic shock → fluids
  4. Wounds
  5. Trauma / multiple trauma

Airway Management (Escalating Order)

  1. Head-tilt/chin-lift; jaw-thrust (C-spine protection)
  2. OPA (oropharyngeal airway) — unconscious, spontaneously breathing; measures lip to ear
  3. NPA (nasopharyngeal airway) — DO NOT USE with facial trauma or basal skull fracture
  4. Endotracheal intubation (ETT)
  5. King Tube / LMA — field airway
  6. Cricothyroidotomy — emergency when ETT impossible (maxillofacial trauma, C-spine injury, laryngeal edema/spasm); replaced with tracheostomy when stable

Airway obstruction signs: Cannot speak/breathe/cough; stridor; labored breathing; accessory muscle use; nasal flaring

Cyanosis and LOC = LATE signs — do not wait for these to intervene

2.3 Hemorrhage, Trauma & Multiple Injury

▼

Signs of Internal Hemorrhage

  • Tachycardia, ↓BP, thirst, apprehension, cool/moist skin, delayed cap refill, ↓urine output

Hemorrhage Management

  • Two large-bore IVs — preferred in uninjured extremity
  • LR preferred crystalloid for trauma (NS → hyperchloremic acidosis)
  • Blood: type/cross; PRBCs for massive hemorrhage + platelets + FFP
  • Warm blood products for large volumes — cold blood → cardiac arrest/coagulopathy
  • Direct firm pressure; tourniquet when pressure fails
  • Document tourniquet time — label with "T" + time applied; leave until OR

Multiple Trauma

  • Single catastrophic event → life-threatening injuries to ≥2 organs/systems
  • "Triad of death": Hypothermia + Acidosis + Coagulopathy
  • Assume C-spine injury until proven otherwise
  • Remove backboard ASAP (once stabilized) — prolonged backboard = pressure injury

Priority Management for Multiple Injuries

  1. Airway + ventilation
  2. Control hemorrhage
  3. Prevent/treat hypovolemic shock
  4. Head/neck assessment
  5. Evaluate other injuries
  6. Splint fractures; reassess neurovascular status
  7. Ongoing exam; diagnostics

Abdominal Injuries

  • Penetrating: surgery usually required; small bowel = most common hollow organ injured
  • Blunt: immobilize; FAST ultrasound; assess bowel sounds (absent = peritoneal involvement)
  • Evisceration: sterile moist saline dressing; hold oral fluids; NG tube; antibiotics

Rhabdomyolysis

  • Muscle damage → myoglobin → ATN/AKI
  • Classic triad: myalgias, muscle weakness, dark (cola-colored) urine
  • Serum CK >6000 IU/L = diagnostic
  • Fasciotomy for compartment syndrome

2.4 Environmental Emergencies

▼

Heat Stroke

  • Failure of heat-regulating mechanisms
  • Manifestations: CNS dysfunction, T ≥40.6°C (105°F), HOT DRY SKIN (anhidrosis), tachypnea, hypotension, tachycardia
  • Pulse ox NOT reliable; skin color NOT reliable

Management:

  • ABCs + cool to 39.2°C (102°F) as quickly as possible
  • Methods: cool sheets/towels, ice to neck/groin/chest/axillae, cooling blankets, cold water immersion
  • Stop cooling at 38°C (100.4°F) — prevent iatrogenic hypothermia
  • IVF: NS or LR; 100% O&sub2;; continuous rectal/bladder/esophageal temp monitoring
  • Monitor ECG, CVP, LOC, urine output
Exam Focus

Heat stroke = hot DRY skin (anhidrosis — sweat mechanism fails). Heat exhaustion has wet skin. Stop cooling at 38°C — 39.2°C is the target to reach, not where you stop.

Frostbite

  • Controlled rapid rewarming in 37–40°C circulating bath for 30–40 minute intervals
  • DO NOT massage or handle tissue; DO NOT walk if feet are affected
  • Discharge: avoid tobacco, alcohol, caffeine (all vasoconstrictors)

Hypothermia (Core Temp ≤35°C)

  • Risk: elderly, infants, homeless, trauma victims, alcohol intoxication
  • Hypothermia takes precedence over frostbite — treat systemic before localized
  • Active core rewarming: warm IV fluids, warm humidified O&sub2;, warm peritoneal lavage, cardiopulmonary bypass
  • CAUTION: Cold blood returning from extremities → lactic acid → core temp drop → arrhythmias

2.5 Poisoning & Overdose

▼

General Goals (in order)

  1. Control airway first
  2. Remove/inactivate poison before absorbed
  3. Supportive care
  4. Specific antidotes
  5. Hasten elimination

Decontamination Methods

  • Activated charcoal: Slurry orally or by NGT; NOT for: corrosives, heavy metals, hydrocarbons, iron, lithium; do NOT give with NAC
  • Gastric lavage: Only useful within 1 hour; for sustained-release or massive ingestion
  • Ipecac: NOT recommended; NEVER with corrosives or petroleum distillates

Antidotes & Treatment

ToxinAntidote/TreatmentKey Notes
Opioids Naloxone (Narcan) IV/IM Watch for relapse — naloxone wears off faster than heroin. Never leave unattended.
Acetaminophen NAC (N-acetylcysteine) #1 overdosed drug; do NOT give activated charcoal with NAC
Nerve agents Atropine + pralidoxime + benzodiazepines Cholinergic excess (SLUDGE); decontaminate first
Cyanide Amyl nitrate / sodium nitrite / sodium thiocyanate / hydroxocobalamin Inhibits aerobic metabolism; bright red skin
Corrosives Dilution: milk or water NEVER induce vomiting; NEVER gastric lavage
Carbon monoxide 100% O&sub2; (or hyperbaric) Pulse ox UNRELIABLE; skin color UNRELIABLE; treat until carboxyhemoglobin <5%

Opioid OD Pattern

  • Pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression, pulmonary edema, coma
  • Give naloxone; watch for relapse (shorter half-life than heroin)
  • Never leave patient unattended

2.6 Violence, Abuse & Special Situations

▼

Sexual Assault

  • Never leave alone; privacy essential
  • All nursing interventions oriented toward: patient gaining sense of control over their life
  • Paper bags (NOT plastic); chain of custody documentation
  • Water-moistened speculum — lubricant interferes with forensic testing
  • STI prophylaxis; emergency contraception within 72h
  • Psychiatric consult if suicide attempt

Human Trafficking

  • Offer to speak alone; ask: "Are you in control of your money?"
  • National Human Trafficking Hotline as resource

Suicidal Patients

  • Direct question: "Have you ever thought about taking your own life?"
  • Never leave alone

Alcohol Withdrawal

  • DTs start 48–96h after last drink; life-threatening if untreated
  • CIWA-Ar protocol for assessment and management
  • Private, well-lit, quiet room; avoid TV/radio (sensory overstimulation triggers seizures)

3.1 Disaster Triage — NATO / MCI System

▼
Exam Focus — Core Distinction

ED Triage: Most critically ill → most resources (regardless of outcome)

Disaster Triage: Greatest good for greatest number → likelihood of survival determines allocation. Expectant (black) = a conscious patient who CANNOT survive even with treatment.

TagPriorityCriteriaExamples
● Red 1 — Immediate Life-threatening but survivable with minimal intervention Sucking chest wound, airway obstruction, shock, tension pneumothorax, burns 15–40% TBSA
● Yellow 2 — Delayed Significant injury; can wait hours without threat to life/limb Stable abdominal wounds, soft tissue injuries, most CNS/eye injuries
● Green 3 — Minimal Minor injuries; treatment can wait hours to days Upper extremity fractures, minor burns, sprains, small lacerations
● Black 4 — Expectant Extensive injuries; survival unlikely even with definitive care; separated but NOT abandoned; comfort only Unresponsive + penetrating head wound, high spinal injury, 3rd-degree burns >60% TBSA, agonal respirations, no pulse/BP, fixed/dilated pupils
Prof Emphasis

Greatest good for greatest number — burns >60% TBSA = Black tag even if the patient is conscious and talking. This feels morally difficult but is the correct disaster triage decision. Know it.

Exam Focus

Red tag = survivable with MINIMAL intervention — not maximum care. Patients who need extensive resources to survive get Black-tagged in disaster triage to preserve resources for multiple Red-tag survivors.

3.2 Emergency Operations Plan (EOP)

▼
  • Joint Commission requires hospitals to create and practice disaster plan twice per year
  • Self-sufficiency goal: Sustain core services without external assistance for at least 96 hours (ideally 7 days)

EOP Components

  • Activation response
  • Internal and external communication plan
  • Coordinated patient care plan
  • Security plans
  • External resource identification
  • People management and traffic flow

EOP Phases

  1. Pre-incident: Preparation, drills, training
  2. Incident response: Active disaster management
  3. Demobilization: Return to normal daily functioning
  4. Post-incident: Critique and debriefing of ALL involved
Exam Focus — Phase Definitions

Demobilization = return to normal daily functioning (NOT drills, NOT resource restocking)

Post-incident = critique and debriefing (NOT conducting drills, NOT replacing resources)

Nurse's Role in Disaster

  • May perform duties outside usual scope (intubation, chest tube, suturing, debridement)
  • May serve as triage officer
  • Know your state's emergency practice regulations

3.4 Personal Protective Equipment (PPE Levels)

▼
LevelRespiratorySkin/EyeUsed When
Level A SCBA (highest respiratory) Vapor-tight chemical-resistant suit Highest protection from ALL routes; most dangerous environments
Level B SCBA (highest respiratory) Chemical-resistant suit (NOT vapor-tight) Highest respiratory protection; lesser skin hazard
Level C Air-purified respirator (filtered) Coverall with splash hood, gloves, boots Used in hospitals; known concentrations + adequate filtration
Level D None Standard work uniform Used in hospitals; low-risk situations
Exam Focus — PPE Distinctions

Levels A and B both use SCBA. The difference is the suit: A = vapor-tight (maximum skin protection), B = NOT vapor-tight.

Level B = highest respiratory protection; Level A = highest protection overall (all routes).

Level C = hospital standard for chemical exposure — air-purified respirator, not SCBA.

Decontamination

  • Remove clothing and decontaminate as close to the scene as possible
  • Soap and water effective for most chemical agents
  • Staff must wear PPE during decontamination
  • Contain and properly dispose of decontamination runoff
  • Don PPE BEFORE contact with contaminated patient

3.5 Biological Weapons

▼
CategoryMortalityExamples
Category A High Anthrax, Botulism, Tularemia, Smallpox, Plague, Viral hemorrhagic fevers (Ebola)
Category B Low/moderate morbidity Brucellosis, Q fever, Typhus, Food poisoning agents
Category C Low Hantavirus
Prof Emphasis

Know the clinical manifestations of Category A — especially Anthrax, Botulism, Smallpox, and Ebola.

Anthrax (High-Yield)

Exam Focus — Anthrax Precautions

Anthrax is NOT spread person-to-person → Standard Precautions ONLY. No contact/droplet/airborne precautions needed. Distractors try to add extra isolation — wrong answer.

  • Routes: Cutaneous (most common — painless black eschar), ingestion, inhalation (most lethal)

Inhalation anthrax stages:

  1. Stage 1: Flu-like — cough, headache, fever, weakness, dyspnea, syncope. Notably: NO rhinorrhea
  2. Stage 2 (1–3 days later): Severe respiratory distress, stridor, cyanosis, hypoxia, diaphoresis, shock
  • Hallmark CXR: hemorrhagic mediastinitis
  • Mortality approaches 100% with inhalation anthrax
  • Treatment: Penicillin-sensitive
  • Mass exposure prophylaxis: ciprofloxacin or doxycycline × 60 days
  • After death: cremation required (spores survive for decades)

3.6 Chemical Weapons & Radiation Exposure

▼

Nerve Agents (Sarin, VX, Soman)

  • Mechanism: Cholinesterase inhibitors → cholinergic excess
  • Symptoms: SLUDGE — Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI distress, Emesis + bronchospasm, visual disturbances, seizures, coma
  • Treatment: Atropine + pralidoxime + benzodiazepines; soap and water decontamination

Vesicants — Sulfur Mustard (Blister Agents)

  • Blistering agents: conjunctivitis, bronchitis, partial-to-full-thickness skin burns
  • Treatment: Soap and water decontamination; blot (do NOT rub); dimercaprol for systemic toxicity

Cyanide (Blood Agent)

  • Inhibits aerobic metabolism; bright red skin; → respiratory arrest, then cardiac arrest
  • Treatment: Amyl nitrate, sodium nitrite, sodium thiocyanate, or hydroxocobalamin

Chemical Weapon Properties

  • Volatility: Tendency to become vapor; most chemicals heavier than air → stand up to reduce exposure
  • Persistence: Weaponized agents more persistent and penetrating than industrial chemicals
  • Latency: Sulfur mustard/pulmonary agents = longest delay; nerve agents/cyanide = seconds

Radiation Exposure — Three Types

TypeDefinitionDecontamination?
External irradiation Exposed to radiation source; patient is not radioactive None needed; not a medical emergency by itself
Contamination Radioactive gases/liquids/solids on body or clothing Immediate decontamination required
Incorporation Uptake of radioactive material into body cells/tissues/organs Requires medical management; cannot be fully decontaminated externally

Exposure affected by: time, distance, shielding

3.7 Blast Injuries — Four Phases

▼
PhaseMechanismInjuries
Primary Initial blast / air overpressure wave Air-filled organs: ears (tympanic membrane), lungs, GI tract
Secondary Debris and shrapnel propelled as projectiles Penetrating injuries, fractures, amputations
Tertiary Pressure wave throws victims through the air Head injuries, fractures, spinal injuries
Quaternary Pre-existing conditions exacerbated OR post-blast complications Asthma/COPD exacerbation, DM crisis, HTN crisis; burns, crush injury, head injuries
Exam Focus — Blast Injury Phases

Primary = air wave → air-filled organs (ears, lungs, gut)

Secondary = shrapnel/debris as projectiles

Tertiary = body becomes the projectile (thrown by the wave)

Quaternary = everything else: exacerbations + complications

3.8 Disaster Psychological Response

▼

Victim Phases

  1. Impact/Acute: Shock, disbelief, fear, guilt, anger — or controlled/composed appearance
  2. Denial/Heightened anxiety: PTSD-consistent (flashbacks, sleep disturbances, hyperalertness, psychosomatic complaints)
  3. Reorganization: Puts incident in perspective; some develop chronic stress disorders or phobias

Nursing Actions

  • Encourage return to normal roles when appropriate
  • Active listening; therapeutic presence
  • Refer to CISM or counseling for persistent symptoms

4.1 Burns — Critical Facts

▼
Most Tested Distractor

Fluid timing starts at TIME OF INJURY — not ED arrival. Every exam has a version of this question.

Fluid / Labs
  • Formula: 2 mL × kg × %TBSA thermal; 4 mL × kg × %TBSA electrical
  • LR ONLY — no NS (hyperchloremic acidosis)
  • NEVER add KCl — already hyperkalemic
  • Emergent labs: ↑K¹, ↓Na¹, ↑Hct
  • Diuresis at 48+ hours = EXPECTED (not AKI)
  • UO is gold standard; BP/HR unreliable
Wound Care
  • Mafenide = ONLY eschar-penetrating topical
  • Wet-to-dry = WRONG for burns
  • Check peripheral pulses post-dressing
  • Xenograft separating = trim away (normal)
  • Homograft + purulent = notify provider
  • Pressure garments = 24h/day
Airway
  • Obstruction can take UP TO 48H to develop
  • Stridor/dyspnea = ominous late signs
  • Airway > fluid > pain — always
  • Give O&sub2; even if SpO&sub2; normal
  • SpO&sub2; unreliable with CO poisoning
Nutrition & Rehab
  • Carbohydrates = most important energy source
  • Enteral preferred; jejunal feedings common
  • Weight loss = fat catabolism, NOT fluid loss
  • PTSD = most common psych complication (up to 45%)
  • Reconstruction after scars mature
Population
  • Children and elderly = highest morbidity/mortality
  • Age matters: 4-year-old 24% > 39-year-old 18%
  • First systemic event = hemodynamic instability
  • Burn shock = hypovolemic type initially
Rule of Nines
  • Head: 9%
  • Each arm: 9%
  • Anterior trunk: 18%
  • Posterior trunk: 18%
  • Each leg: 18%
  • Perineum: 1%

4.2 Emergency Nursing — Critical Facts

▼
Triage
  • Disaster triage ≠ ED triage — largest conceptual difference tested
  • 75% TBSA full-thickness = Black tag even if conscious
  • Red tag = survivable with MINIMAL intervention
  • ESI = acuity PLUS anticipated resource needs
Environmental
  • Heat stroke = HOT DRY skin (anhidrosis)
  • Stop cooling at 38°C (not 39.2°C)
  • Hypothermia takes precedence over frostbite
  • SpO&sub2; unreliable in heat stroke
Poisoning
  • CO: pulse ox unreliable; skin color unreliable
  • Corrosives: NEVER vomiting; NEVER lavage
  • Charcoal NOT for: corrosives, iron, lithium, heavy metals, hydrocarbons
  • Naloxone: watch for relapse
  • Nerve agent → atropine
  • Cyanide → amyl nitrate/hydroxocobalamin
Trauma / Other
  • LR preferred over NS in trauma
  • Warm blood products for large volumes
  • Document tourniquet time; label with "T"
  • Paper bags for evidence (NOT plastic)
  • All sexual assault interventions = patient regaining control
  • DTs: 48–96h after last drink; quiet well-lit room

4.3 Disaster Nursing — Critical Facts

▼
PPE
  • Level A = highest ALL routes (vapor-tight)
  • Level B = highest respiratory (SCBA); NOT vapor-tight skin
  • Level C = air-purified respirator; hospital use
  • Level D = standard uniform; low risk
Anthrax
  • Standard precautions ONLY (not contagious)
  • Hallmark CXR: hemorrhagic mediastinitis
  • No rhinorrhea in Stage 1 (flu-like)
  • Mass prophylaxis: cipro or doxy × 60 days
  • Cremation required (spores survive decades)
Blast Injuries
  • Primary = air wave → air-filled organs
  • Secondary = shrapnel/debris projectiles
  • Tertiary = body becomes projectile
  • Quaternary = exacerbations + complications
EOP / CISM
  • Self-sufficiency goal = 96 hours minimum
  • Demobilization = return to normal functioning
  • Post-incident = critique/debriefing (not drills)
  • CISM = recognize stress reactions (not evaluate performance)
  • Joint Commission: disaster drill 2× per year
Chemical Agents
  • Nerve agents → SLUDGE → atropine + pralidoxime
  • Vesicants → blister; blot, do NOT rub
  • Cyanide → bright red skin → amyl nitrate
  • Most chemicals heavier than air → stand up
  • Sulfur mustard = longest latency
